Today's sky, one probe, six tries

Every day the solar system is placed where the planets really are on that date, and one of them is the target. Your job is to launch a probe from Earth that gets captured there. You have two dials and six launches. Everyone in the world gets the same board, so the score you share is a fair comparison.

Nothing here is on rails. The launch is flown under full N-body gravity — the same physics as our Solar System simulator — so Jupiter tugs on your probe, a near pass of Venus bends it, and the target keeps moving while you're in flight.

The two dials

Speed over Earth

  • How hard the probe is pushed, in km/s beyond Earth's own 30 km/s around the Sun.
  • Mars needs a few km/s. The giants need about ten. Falling in to Mercury takes a hard braking burn.
  • Too little and the probe never reaches the target's orbit; too much and it sails past it early.

Aim

  • The heading of the push. 0° is straight along Earth's direction of travel — the cheapest way outward.
  • Positive angles swing the push away from the Sun, negative toward it.
  • ±180° is a braking burn against Earth's motion: the probe slows down and falls inward toward Venus or Mercury.

The dotted line is a preview of the probe's path around the Sun alone, so you can see whether it reaches the target's orbit. What the preview can't show is the puzzle: where the planet will be when you get there. After each launch the result card tells you how far ahead or behind the target was when you crossed its orbit — that's your clue. Planet ahead? You were late: more speed. Planet behind? You were early: ease off.

Par, fuel and stars

Par is the cheapest launch the physics can find for today: hundreds of candidate transfers are computed, and the best few are flown for real in the simulation until one arrives. Fuel is counted the way mission planners count it — the launch burn, plus any mid-course correction, plus the braking burn at the planet. Your probe carries a small correction tank (1.5 km/s): if a launch would miss by a little, it fires one Voyager-style course correction on the way, and that fuel counts against you.

  • ★★★ — arrived within 5% of par's fuel
  • ★★ — within 25%
  • ★ — arrived, whatever it cost

Six misses and the planet gets away. When the day is over you see par's own path as a ghost line, its dial settings, and the porkchop strip: fuel against flight time for every route the scan found, so you can see where your launch sat on the curve.

Questions

Is the solar system really where it is today?

Yes. Each day the planets are placed from their real orbital elements for that date, so Earth and the target sit where they do in the sky tonight, and the launch flies under full N-body gravity. Everyone gets the same board on the same UTC date; a new one appears at 00:00 UTC.

Why did my probe miss when the path looked right?

Because the planet keeps moving. Reaching the target's orbit is the easy half; the hard half is arriving at the moment the planet does. Real Mars missions launch only every 26 months for exactly this reason — the geometry has to line up. Here the date is fixed, so you tune the timing with speed instead.

Why is some days' par so expensive?

Because the launch window is closed. The cheapest route to a planet only exists when it sits at the right angle ahead of Earth; on other days the physics still finds a way, but it costs more and arrives fast. Par is always measured on today's board, so stars are fair on a bad day and a good one.

What are the drawn sizes and distances?

Orbits and positions are real, in AU. Planets are drawn far larger than life so you can see them, and on the outer-planet boards the spacing is gently compressed so Earth's orbit stays visible next to Saturn's. The capture ring around the target is enlarged too: the real one is a slice of the planet's gravitational sphere of influence.

Do I lose my streak if I miss a day?

Yes — it counts consecutive days on which the probe arrived. It lives in this browser's local storage; nothing is uploaded and there is no account.

Is it free?

Yes — free, in the browser, no download and no signup.
