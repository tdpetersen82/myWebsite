The gesture code

  • Fingers held up — number of words · then a finger per word you’re acting
  • Fingers on your forearm — syllables in that word
  • Tug your earlobe — "sounds like…"
  • Pinch finger and thumb — a small word (the, a, of)
  • Beckon — "keep guessing, warmer" · palm out — "stop, colder"
  • Never talk, never mouth words, never point at the answer

How to play charades

Two teams, one silent actor, one prompt from the bowl. The actor signals the category and word count with the gesture code, then performs — whole prompt or word by word — while their team shouts guesses. A correct guess inside the time limit scores; then the other team takes the stage. No sounds, no lip-movement, no spelling in the air: the silence is the game.

Why the gesture code is printed

Charades collapses when the actor and the guessers speak different sign languages. The code on the sheet — word counts on raised fingers, syllables on the forearm, the earlobe tug for "sounds like" — is the traditional shared vocabulary, and having it visible turns first-timers into players in one round. House additions (the "small word" pinch, hot-and-cold palms) are printed too because everyone half-remembers them differently.

Making the card bowl

Cut the twenty-four cards along their dashed edges, fold once, and mix in a bowl. The mix is tuned for acting range — a flamingo asks different muscles than a washing machine — and deliberately avoids movie titles, so nobody's trivia gap becomes their team's problem. Prefer drawing to acting? The word lists page feeds the pencil-and-paper version.

Frequently asked

Can the actor nod yes and no? Yes — responding to guesses with nods and the hot/cold palms is standard. Mouthing words is not.

Teams uneven? Rotate a permanent guesser to whichever team is acting — everyone plays, nobody sits.
