Poker hands in order

Every standard poker game — Texas Hold'em, five-card draw, video poker, seven-card stud — uses the same ten-hand ranking. Learn it once and it transfers everywhere. The order is driven purely by rarity: the harder a hand is to make, the more it beats.

How ties are broken

When two players hold the same category of hand, the highest card within the hand decides it. Two flushes? The one with the highest card wins, then the second highest, and so on. Two pair? Compare the higher pair first, then the lower pair, then the kicker.

Suits never break ties. There is no "spades beat hearts" rule in standard poker — if two hands are genuinely identical, the pot is split.

Only five cards count

In Texas Hold'em you see seven cards, but your hand is the best five of them. If the board plays better than your hole cards, the board plays — and if it plays better for everyone, the pot splits.

The rules people get wrong

A-2-3-4-5 is a straight. Known as the wheel or the bicycle, it is the lowest possible straight — the ace plays low, so it loses to 2-3-4-5-6.

Straights do not wrap around. Q-K-A-2-3 is not a straight. The ace can be high or low, never both at once.

A flush beats a straight. This trips up new players constantly. There are simply fewer ways to make a flush than a straight, so it ranks higher.

Three of a kind beats two pair. Also purely a rarity question, and also frequently misremembered at kitchen tables.

Frequently asked

What is the best hand in poker? A royal flush — ace, king, queen, jack, ten, all in one suit. It is the top end of the straight-flush category rather than a separate hand.

Does a full house beat a flush? Yes. Full house is the fourth-strongest hand, flush is fifth.

What beats four of a kind? Only a straight flush or a royal flush.
