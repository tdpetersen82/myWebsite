Algebraic notation in one minute

  • Pieces: King · Queen · Rook · Bishop · N = knight · pawns get no letter
  • Squares: files a–h from White’s left, ranks 1–8 from White’s side — "Nf3" = knight to f3, "e4" = pawn to e4
  • x capture (exd5) · O-O castle short · O-O-O castle long
  • + check · # checkmate · = promotion (e8=Q)
  • Two identical pieces can reach the square? Add the file: Rad1
Fifty moves covers the great majority of games; a marathon continues onto a second sheet from move 51. In rated over-the-board play, keeping score is required while you have more than five minutes on the clock — check your event’s rules.

How to record a chess game

Write each move as it happens, in ink, before you press the clock — White's move in the left column, Black's beside it. The language is algebraic notation, and it's smaller than it looks: name the piece, name the square. Knight to f3 is Nf3. Pawn to e4 is just e4. Add an x for a capture, a + for check, and you already read 95% of every chess book ever printed.

Why bother at a kitchen table

Because the score sheet is a mirror. Replaying your own game an hour later — why did I move the queen there? — teaches more than a dozen puzzle apps, and it's the habit that separates players who improve from players who repeat. It also makes the win permanent: a filled score sheet from a hard-fought game is a better trophy than most trophies.

Practicing the notation

The one-minute box on the sheet covers the whole grammar, including the awkward cases — two rooks reaching the same square (Rad1), promotion (e8=Q), the two castlings. Want to drill it? Record a game against the computer here, then replay it from your own sheet. The printable board pairs well for analysis away from the screen.

Frequently asked

Do I write my opponent's moves too? Yes — both columns, always. The sheet records the game, not just your half of it.

What if I fall behind? Catch up on your own time, copying from your opponent's sheet if needed — standard practice even in tournaments.
