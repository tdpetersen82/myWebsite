Running it clean

  • Rider draws (or is dealt) one card per checkpoint — record it on the sheet immediately, initialed by the stop marshal
  • All five stops = a five-card hand · best poker hand wins the pot
  • Common variants: seven stops, keep the best five · buy an extra card at the finish · worst hand takes a consolation prize
  • Ties: highest card in the hand wins, or split — announce before the run
Poker runs are charity-event folklore, and formats drift: sealed envelopes per stop, dealt-on-the-spot cards, dice versions for short courses. This sheet is the common denominator — one recorded card per stop, rankings at the finish. Check local rules where wagers are involved.

How to run a poker run

Map a route with five stops. At each, a marshal deals or lets each rider draw one card, records it on the sheet (initials prevent finish-line creativity), and waves them on. When the last rider clears the last stop, every line on the sheet reads as a five-card poker hand — best hand takes the pot, and by hallowed tradition the worst hand takes home something embarrassing.

Why the sheet matters more than the cards

Poker runs live or die on record-keeping: cards get lost in jacket pockets, memories improve with beer, and the pot is real money for a real charity. One master sheet — or one sheet per stop, merged at the finish — with initials at every box is the entire integrity system. The rankings table is printed beside the rows because the finish line is exactly where "does two pair beat three of a kind?" gets asked, loudly.

Formats that work

The classic is five stops, five cards. Bigger runs deal seven and keep the best five (the sheet's extra columns absorb it — relabel two). Fundraisers sell extra cards at the finish, which is painless profit. And the format is vehicle-agnostic — the same sheet has run motorcycles, pontoons and office pub crawls without changing a line. Brush up before the run with the hand rankings chart or a few hands of hold'em.

Frequently asked

Is a poker run gambling? Where entry fees and pots are involved, local rules apply — most jurisdictions have charity-event provisions. The organizer's job is to check first.

Sealed envelopes or open draws? Sealed cards opened at the finish add suspense and prevent mid-route despair; open draws are more social. Both are standard.
